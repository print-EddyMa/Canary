# Keyless open data carries Canary's 48-hour build

A team can build Canary's required core and a defensible version of all five stretch layers in 48 hours using free sources, most of which need no login at all. The backbone is two keyless point APIs that return decades of data per click: **NASA POWER gave 45 years (1981-2025) of monthly MERRA-2 values in 0.53 seconds**, and **Open-Meteo gave 86 years (1940-2026) of daily ERA5 values in 5.5 seconds**. Satellite-observed vital signs (GRACE water storage, MODIS vegetation, land temperature, snow) need a free Earthdata Login or Google Earth Engine, and the most valuable of them, the GRACE mascon record, is a single 44 MB file. On the Google side the dividing line is sharp: Earth Engine's free tier, AlphaEarth embeddings, ERA5 cloud stores, TimesFM 2.5 and the Gemini API are open today, while WeatherNext forecast data, the Earth AI geospatial agent, the Flood Forecasting API and FireSat are gated behind approvals that take longer than a weekend. The statistics themselves are cheap: a global 0.5 degree Mann-Kendall plus Theil-Sen map ran in 29 seconds in the researchers' own benchmark. The scientific risk sits elsewhere: in the same benchmark an uncorrected trend test flagged 15% of pure-noise cells, and the naive significance test for critical slowing down flagged 80%. The research was stopped early at the user's request, so this report separates what was probed live on 14 November 2026 from what rests on search summaries or prior knowledge; the official Resources tab of the challenge page was never retrieved, no Earthdata download was timed, and several details the stretch layers depend on (the GRACE mission gap, Earth Engine dataset IDs, Gemini free-tier limits, PCMCI runtime) remain unverified.

Every claim below carries one of six evidence labels, taken from the research notes and applied consistently.

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[live]** | A researcher called the endpoint or read the file on 14 Nov 2026 and reports what came back |
| **[CMR]** | Read from NASA's Common Metadata Repository on 14 Nov 2026 (collection span, latest granule, granule size) |
| **[page]** | The cited page or package registry entry was opened and read |
| **[snippet]** | Seen only in a search-result summary; the page itself was not opened. Probable, not confirmed |
| **[measured]** | Timed or tested by the researchers in a throwaway environment; no external source exists |
| **[unverified]** | Researcher's prior knowledge, not re-checked in this session. Do not put on a slide without checking |

## NASA POWER answers in half a second; satellite records need an Earthdata Login

The challenge's core (pick variables, build a regional series, compute a rate of change and its significance) is satisfied fastest by NASA POWER. One unauthenticated GET to the monthly point endpoint returned 45 years of temperature and precipitation for a test point at 28.6N, 77.2E as 17 KB of JSON in 0.53 seconds, with the response header reporting source MERRA-2, 1 January 1981 to 31 December 2025 **[live]** ([POWER monthly point API](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/monthly/point?parameters=T2M,PRECTOTCORR&community=AG&longitude=77.2&latitude=28.6&start=1981&end=2025&format=JSON)). The parameter catalogue returned 1,388 monthly names, and the researchers confirmed the ones Canary needs: `T2M`, `T2M_MAX`, `T2M_MIN` and `TS` in degrees C, `PRECTOTCORR` in mm/day, soil wetness `GWETTOP`, `GWETROOT` and `GWETPROF` as 0-1 fractions, humidity, wind and pressure, surface shortwave and longwave radiation, `CLOUD_AMT`, snow depth, land evaporation `EVLAND`, total ozone `TO3` and aerosol optical depth `AOD_55` **[live]** ([POWER parameter manager](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/system/manager/parameters?community=AG&temporal=MONTHLY)). A point request takes up to 20 parameters; meteorology starts in 1981 and solar in 1984; output comes as JSON, CSV, ASCII or NetCDF **[page]** ([POWER monthly API docs](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/services/api/temporal/monthly/)).

POWER has four gotchas that will bite in the first hour. Monthly keys are `YYYYMM` and month `13` is the annual value, which must be stripped before fitting a trend, and the fill value is -999 **[live]**. The regional endpoint returns the native 0.5 by 0.625 degree grid but rejects any box wider than 10 degrees with HTTP 422 and accepts one parameter per request; a 10 by 10 degree box for 45 years came back as 1.56 MB in 2.3 seconds **[live]** ([POWER monthly regional API](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/monthly/regional?latitude-min=20&latitude-max=30&longitude-min=70&longitude-max=80&parameters=T2M&community=AG&start=1981&end=2025&format=CSV)). The monthly record stops at December 2025; the daily endpoint does reach November 2026 with a two-to-five-day lag, but it reports sources GEOS-IT and FLASHFLUX rather than MERRA-2, so extending a series into 2026 splices two systems **[live]** ([POWER daily point API](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/api/temporal/daily/point?parameters=T2M,PRECTOTCORR,ALLSKY_SFC_SW_DWN,GWETROOT&community=AG&longitude=77.2&latitude=28.6&start=20261025&end=20261113&format=JSON)). Finally, the API returns HTTP 429 when overused but publishes no numeric rate limit on the pages read **[page]** ([POWER API overview](https://power.larc.nasa.gov/docs/services/api/)). For regions larger than 10 degrees there is a second no-auth route: POWER publishes global monthly, daily and hourly Zarr stores in the anonymous S3 bucket `nasa-power`. The researchers listed the bucket and read the store attributes but did not time a data read **[live, listing only]** ([nasa-power S3 bucket listing](https://nasa-power.s3.amazonaws.com/?list-type=2&delimiter=/&prefix=merra2/temporal/)).

Two global headline series also need no login. GISTEMP v4 global land-ocean monthly anomalies download as a 13 KB CSV running from 1880 through October 2026 **[live]** ([GISTEMP GLB.Ts+dSST.csv](https://data.giss.nasa.gov/gistemp/tabledata_v4/GLB.Ts+dSST.csv)). The NSIDC Sea Ice Index is now version 4.0, one small CSV per month and hemisphere from 1979 through September 2026; the old `_v3.0.csv` filenames return 404 **[live]** ([NSIDC G02135 monthly CSV](https://noaadata.apps.nsidc.org/NOAA/G02135/north/monthly/data/N_09_extent_v4.0.csv)).

### The 2025-2026 changes that break old tutorials

Several records changed under the team's feet in the past eighteen months, and each is a potential fake trend. IMERG's V07 Final record is frozen at September 2025 while its parent products move to V08; as of 6 August 2026 V08 Final was unreleased and the Early and Late runs were in a "hybrid" mode **[page]** ([GPM: update to IMERG V08 transition schedule, Aug 2026](https://gpm.nasa.gov/data/news/update-imerg-v08-transition-schedule-aug-2026)), and CMR still showed only V07 ending 2025-09 on 14 November **[CMR]** ([CMR GPM_3IMERGM](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/collections.json?short_name=GPM_3IMERGM)). Terra stopped inclination manoeuvres in late 2020 and by fall 2022 was crossing the equator at 10:15 instead of 10:30, which matters for any time-of-day-sensitive variable such as land surface temperature; science operations are projected to end for Terra in February 2027, Aqua in September 2027 and Aura in August 2028 **[page]** ([NASA Science: Terra, the end of an era](https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/earth-science/terra-the-end-of-an-era)). MODIS monthly products were nonetheless still flowing through October 2026 **[CMR]**, which makes the 2000-2026 MODIS series the longest single-sensor land record available and removes any need for a VIIRS splice this weekend. Suomi NPP granules stop at 1-2 November 2026 in CMR **[CMR]** ([CMR VNP46A2](https://cmr.earthdata.nasa.gov/search/collections.json?short_name=VNP46A2)), consistent with a reported end of new S-NPP product delivery **[snippet]** ([Earthdata tools listing](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/tools?page=2)); every `VNP*` product is now a closed record.

The Sea Ice Index switched its input product inside the series: the `source_dataset` column reads `NSIDC-0051` for early rows and `NSIDC-0803` for 2025 and 2026 **[live]** ([NSIDC G02135 monthly CSV](https://noaadata.apps.nsidc.org/NOAA/G02135/north/monthly/data/N_09_extent_v4.0.csv)). SEDAC was shut down in 2025 and its datasets, including GPW, were relocated to Earthdata Search and frozen **[snippet]** ([Earthdata: SEDAC data back in Earthdata Search](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/alerts-outages/sedac-data-back-earthdata-search)). LP DAAC retired Data Pool distribution for some products in December 2025, the LAADS Classic API is being retired, and all DAAC websites are being folded into earthdata.nasa.gov by the end of 2026 **[snippet]** ([LP DAAC Data Pool notice](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/alerts-outages/lp-daac-discontinues-data-pool-distribution-aster-gdem-measures-community); [LAADS Classic API retirement](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/data/alerts-outages/retirement-laads-classic-api-transition-laads-api-v2); [Web Unification Project](https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/about/web-unification-project)). NISAR data became public in 2026 in three maturity levels (BETA, PROVISIONAL, VALIDATED) that must not be mixed, with a permanent instrument gap from 27 July to 10 August 2026 **[page]** ([ASF NISAR availability overview](https://nisar-docs.asf.alaska.edu/availability-overview/)). The lesson for Canary is that code copied from a pre-2025 tutorial will contain dead SEDAC, Data Pool, LAADS and Sea Ice Index v3 links.

One gap in this section outranks the rest. **The official Resources tab of the challenge page was not retrieved**: the page returned HTTP 503 twice and the Wayback Machine was unreachable. The only signal about what NASA recommends is indirect: two participant repositories for this challenge cite NASA POWER, PO.DAAC sea level, GISTEMP, the NSIDC Sea Ice Index and NOAA GML CO2 ([entitySource/trend_detective](https://github.com/entitySource/trend_detective); [GOHSUNJIN/TerraTrend](https://github.com/GOHSUNJIN/TerraTrend)). That is what other teams used, not the official list.

The key insight for Canary's design is that **Google's multi-decade history comes from the reanalysis and NASA archives it hosts, not from its AI models**. WeatherNext, Weather Lab and the Flood Forecasting API are forecast tools with at most a few years of archived forecasts; Google's own observational products span a decade or less (AlphaEarth 2017-2024, Dynamic World from 2015, Open Buildings 2016-2023). Trend and early-warning statistics therefore rest on ERA5 and NASA records; Google's contribution is compute (Earth Engine), a ground-change layer (embeddings) and the forecast and agent layers (TimesFM, Gemini).

AlphaEarth embeddings earn a place as a "what changed on the ground" layer, with limits that must be stated. The documented change recipe is the dot product between two years' embedding images, which for unit vectors is the cosine of the angle between them; similarity search averages dot products against reference pixels and thresholds them (the official tutorial uses 0.90), and k-means clustering runs on a sample of pixels **[page]** ([catalogue: Satellite Embedding V1](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/GOOGLE_SATELLITE_EMBEDDING_V1_ANNUAL); [tutorial 5: similarity search](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/tutorials/community/satellite-embedding-05-similarity-search); [tutorial 1: introduction](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/tutorials/community/satellite-embedding-01-introduction)). With eight annual vectors per pixel the dataset cannot support a multi-decade trend test or any critical-slowing-down statistic. Three documented traps apply: `mosaic()` resets the projection to WGS84 at 1-degree scale, so `crs` and `scale` must be set explicitly **[page]**; the 2017 layer was regenerated in November 2025 with extra Sentinel-1 data and "some large-scale swath and data availability artifacts remain" **[page]**; and a practitioner write-up reports that raw embedding distances "do not live on a consistent scale across pixels", which argues for scoring each pixel's change against its own history rather than a global threshold **[snippet]** ([Wherobots: AlphaEarth change detection part 2](https://wherobots.com/blog/alphaearth-foundations-change-detection-part-2/)). An independent NeurIPS 2026 audit concludes, per its abstract, that annual embeddings work as portable change signals but do not replace task-specific indices **[snippet]** ([NeurIPS 2026 poster 157096](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/157096)).

Licences are permissive for everything reachable, with five caveats. TimesFM 3.0 weights are non-commercial and non-production. NeuralGCM weights are share-alike. WeatherNext and PARDIG data must carry wording that they are "intended for experimental modelling only" and not validated for real-world use **[page]** ([catalogue: WeatherNext 2](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/datasets/catalog/projects_gcp-public-data-weathernext_assets_weathernext_2_0_0)). Real-time WeatherNext data falls under separate experimental terms that were not read. And the legacy GraphCast and GenCast weights carried, from the researcher's memory, a non-commercial licence that the renamed repository no longer mentions; this is unresolved. Whether a hackathon team counts as "noncommercial" under the Earth Engine questionnaire is likely yes, but no explicit statement was found.

## Outside NASA and Google: ERA5 back to 1940, global grids under 600 MB, indices as plain text

### Open-Meteo is the fastest long daily record for a single place

Open-Meteo's Historical Weather API serves ERA5 (0.25 degree, 1940 to five days ago) and ERA5-Land (0.1 degree, from 1950) with no key for non-commercial use **[page]** ([Open-Meteo Historical Weather API docs](https://open-meteo.com/en/docs/historical-weather-api)). ### The core: Theil-Sen slope, autocorrelation-corrected test, false discovery control

The defensible minimum recipe has four steps. Build monthly anomalies by subtracting each calendar month's climatology over a stated base period, or use annual means. Estimate the slope with Theil-Sen and report it per decade with its confidence interval. Test significance with a Mann-Kendall variant that corrects for autocorrelation, or with an ordinary least-squares t-test using a lag-1 effective sample size. Then, for maps, control the false discovery rate across all grid cells with Benjamini-Hochberg before stippling. This covers all three significance tools the challenge names: t-test, Mann-Kendall and confidence intervals.

`pymannkendall` 1.4.3 (MIT) supplies the named, citable variants: `hamed_rao_modification_test`, `yue_wang_modification_test`, `seasonal_test(period=12)` and pre-whitening tests, each returning trend, p-value, tau and the Theil-Sen slope and intercept in one call ([PyPI pymannkendall](https://pypi.org/project/pymannkendall/)). It has had no release since January 2023 but still imported and ran on Python 3.13 with numpy 2.5.4 **[measured]**. `scipy` 1.18.1 adds `theilslopes`, `linregress`, `kendalltau` and `false_discovery_control(ps, method='bh')`, and has no autocorrelation-corrected Mann-Kendall of its own ([PyPI scipy](https://pypi.org/project/scipy/)). `statsmodels` 0.15.0 supplies `STL`, `MSTL` and `multipletests(..., method='fdr_bh')` ([PyPI statsmodels](https://pypi.org/project/statsmodels/)). For headline regions a change-point check answers "why linear?": `ruptures` 1.1.10 (BSD-2) and `Rbeast` 0.1.25 are current, while Python `bfast` 0.7 is GPL and unmaintained since 2021 ([PyPI ruptures](https://pypi.org/project/ruptures/); [PyPI Rbeast](https://pypi.org/project/Rbeast/); [PyPI bfast](https://pypi.org/project/bfast/)).

The reason for the correction steps is the single most important finding for the judges. Wilks (2016) is the standard reference on gridded significance maps overstating results without multiplicity control, and proposes false discovery rate control as the "statistically principled and computationally easy approach" ([Wilks 2016, BAMS](https://doi.org/10.1175/BAMS-D-15-00267.1)). The researchers reproduced the failure on 259,200 simulated cells of trendless AR(1) noise (phi = 0.3, 276 months): **the naive t-test rejected 15.0% at p < 0.05, the lag-1 corrected test 5.4% (13,950 cells still stippled), and after Benjamini-Hochberg at 0.10 only 2 cells survived [measured]**. Wilks's rule of thumb that the false discovery rate should be set at twice the global level was not confirmed from the paper **[unverified]**, and the Hamed-Rao, Yue-Wang and Santer references were cited from memory by DOI without being reopened.

Compute is not a constraint if per-cell loops are compiled. The table gives the measured budget for a global 0.5 degree grid of 259,200 cells with 276 monthly steps.

### (a) Tipping-point radar: a resilience indicator, never a tipping date

The classical recipe is to take a single-sensor record, remove trend and seasonality with STL, compute lag-1 autocorrelation and variance in a sliding window of about five years, summarise each with Kendall tau against time, and obtain significance from surrogate series. `ewstools` 2.1.3 (MIT, released July 2026) wraps this for individual series: `TimeSeries(data).detrend(...)`, `compute_var`, `compute_auto(lag=1)`, `compute_ktau` and a Plotly plot ([PyPI ewstools](https://pypi.org/project/ewstools/)). For a global map the numba kernels above do the work.

Three criticisms define what Canary can honestly claim. First, the naive test is worthless: on stationary noise, rolling AR(1) showed Kendall tau above 0.3 in 19.6% of cells by chance, and the naive tau p-value flagged **80.2% of null cells** as significant, because overlapping windows are strongly dependent **[measured]**. Surrogates are mandatory, and they are the real compute cost of this layer: about 3.3 seconds per pass at 0.5 degree, so roughly 11 minutes for 200 surrogates and an hour for 1,000 **[extrapolated from measured]**. Second, merged multi-instrument records manufacture the signal: Smith et al. (2023) show that changes in noise structure across sensors propagate into autocorrelation and variance, and that the bias is typically stronger in regional or land-cover aggregates ([Smith et al. 2023, Earth Syst. Dynam.](https://esd.copernicus.org/articles/14/173/2023/)). Third, tipping times cannot be extrapolated: Ben-Yami et al. (2024) found AMOC tipping-time estimates ranging from 2050 to 8065 depending on fingerprint and dataset ([PIK repository copy](https://publications.pik-potsdam.de/pubman/item/item_30214)).

The radar should therefore use MODIS-only vegetation or one GRACE solution, require autocorrelation and variance to rise together, show sensitivity to window length (36, 60 and 84 months), and label its output a "resilience indicator trend". With 20-25 years of monthly data and a five-year window there are only four or five independent windows per cell, so regional aggregates of a single-sensor product are more defensible than a speckled global map. The deep-learning classifier of Bury et al. is a poor fit: its code is CC BY-NC-SA, it was built for 500- or 1,500-point inputs against Canary's 276, and its TensorFlow pin does not match a current Python ([deep-early-warnings-pnas](https://github.com/ThomasMBury/deep-early-warnings-pnas)). The foundational papers the team will want to cite (Scheffer 2009, Dakos 2012, Boulton 2022, Smith 2022, Forzieri 2022) were recalled by DOI and not reopened; their exact STL settings and surrogate methods are **[unverified]**.

### (b) Nature versus humans: a basin-scale residual with wide error bars

The anchor reference is Rodell et al. (2018), which quantified 34 trends in GRACE terrestrial water storage for 2002-2016 and attributed each to natural variability, unsustainable groundwater use, climate change or a combination ([NASA NTRS record](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20180003130); [DOI 10.1038/s41586-018-0123-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0123-1)). That attribution was assembled region by region from several lines of evidence; in Xinjiang, for example, reduced rainfall was not the cause of the loss ([EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/784016)). Canary should adopt the paper's categories plus "mixed" rather than a binary verdict.

The working equation, groundwater change as GRACE storage change minus soil moisture, snow and surface water from a land-surface model, is the researcher's recollection of Rodell et al. (2009) and Famiglietti et al. (2011) and is **[unverified]** ([DOI 10.1038/nature08238](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature08238); [DOI 10.1029/2010GL046442](https://doi.org/10.1029/2010GL046442)). So are the facts that make it fragile: GRACE's effective resolution of about 300 km, the roughly eleven-month gap between GRACE and GRACE-FO in 2017-2018, and the absence of irrigation and pumping in GLDAS Noah, which means the residual mixes human use with model error. What is verified is the data supply: the JPL mascon is one 44 MB file from April 2002 **[CMR]**, GLDAS Noah v2.1 monthly runs from 2000 and must not be spliced with v2.0 **[CMR]**, and GPCP precipitation is a 20 MB download **[live]**. One practical note follows from the confirmed units: POWER's soil wetness is a 0-1 fraction, not a storage depth, so it can show whether soils are drying but cannot be subtracted from GRACE; the residual needs GLDAS storage terms. The defensible presentation works at basin scale, never single pixels, shows the precipitation trend beside the storage trend, and draws error bars.

### (c) Causal discovery: few nodes, named confounders, modest wording

`tigramite` 5.2.10.1 (January 2026, GPL-3.0) is the standard tool ([PyPI tigramite](https://pypi.org/project/tigramite/)). Its README lists the assumptions that judges will probe: PCMCI requires causal stationarity, no contemporaneous links and no hidden variables; PCMCI+ allows contemporaneous links; LPCMCI allows latent confounders; and the default `ParCorr` test assumes linear dependence with Gaussian noise ([tigramite README](https://github.com/jakobrunge/tigramite)). The call pattern is `PCMCI(dataframe=pp.DataFrame(data, var_names=...), cond_ind_test=ParCorr()).run_pcmci(tau_max=..., pc_alpha=...)` followed by `get_corrected_pvalues(..., fdr_method='fdr_bh')` **[measured]**. There is an install trap: after a plain `pip install tigramite`, importing PCMCI fails with `ModuleNotFoundError: No module named 'joblib'`; use `pip install "tigramite[all]"` **[measured]**.

Dimension reduction is not optional. PCMCI on raw grid cells would pit thousands of redundant variables against fewer than 300 time steps, so the graph should have 10-20 nodes: regional means or varimax-rotated modes from `xeofs` 3.0.6 (MIT, active) ([PyPI xeofs](https://pypi.org/project/xeofs/)), plus the ENSO, IOD, NAO, PDO and AMO indices as explicit nodes so that the obvious common driver is in the graph rather than hidden. Inputs must be detrended and deseasonalised, because two trends correlate without any causal link. Results should be worded as "consistent with a causal influence under stated assumptions". PCMCI's runtime was never measured; the expectation of seconds to a minute with `ParCorr` is **[unverified]**, as are the PCMCI method papers, which were cited by DOI from memory.

### (d) Forecasting: Chronos-2 beside mandatory baselines

The two research strands recommend different models, and both are sound. Chronos-2 (Amazon, 120M parameters, Apache-2.0, `chronos-forecasting` 2.3.2) offers a context of 8,192 steps, a horizon of 1,024, quantile output, CPU inference, and native past and known-future covariates through a pandas-in, pandas-out call; `autogluon/chronos-2-small` is a 28M sibling ([amazon/chronos-2 model card](https://huggingface.co/amazon/chronos-2); [PyPI chronos-forecasting](https://pypi.org/project/chronos-forecasting/)). TimesFM 2.5 (Google, 200M parameters, Apache-2.0) has a 16k context that fits 40 years of daily data ([timesfm README](https://github.com/google-research/timesfm)). Chronos-2 is the primary recommendation because it takes covariates such as an ENSO index natively, with quantiles, in one call under a permissive licence. TimesFM 2.5 gained covariate support through an XReg add-on in October 2025, but native multivariate and covariate modelling arrived only in version 3.0, whose weights are non-commercial. TimesFM 2.5 is the natural second model for teams that want a Google element. Models to avoid are Moirai (all checkpoints CC-BY-NC), Lag-Llama (unmaintained since 2024, no pip package) and TimeGPT (closed weights behind an API, per the researcher's recollection) ([HF Salesforce/moirai-2.0-R-small](https://huggingface.co/Salesforce/moirai-2.0-R-small); [HF Lag-Llama](https://huggingface.co/time-series-foundation-models/Lag-Llama)).

The honest evidence is thin. The research found **no head-to-head benchmark of these models on climate variables**, only scattered application studies seen as abstracts; the "state of the art" statement on the Chronos-2 card is the vendor's, and no CPU latency was measured for any model. Any skill claim must therefore come from the team's own rolling-origin backtest against climatology, persistence and seasonal-naive forecasts, using `statsforecast` 2.1.1 baselines (`AutoETS`, `AutoARIMA`, `AutoTheta`, `SeasonalNaive`) that run in seconds ([PyPI statsforecast](https://pypi.org/project/statsforecast/)). The forecast should be presented as a statistical continuation with an uncertainty band, conditional on no regime change, and a model that fails to beat seasonal-naive should be reported as such.

Earth-specific foundation models do not belong in the core. Prithvi-EO-2.0, TerraMind and Clay work on 10-30 m image patches and solve segmentation or regression after fine-tuning; Prithvi WxC is a 2.3-billion-parameter weather model on 6-hourly MERRA-2 fields ([Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M-TL model card](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M-TL); [Prithvi-WxC model card](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-WxC-1.0-2300M)). None consumes a coarse monthly series or produces a trend. Their only cheap use is a side illustration: an existing flood or burn-scar fine-tune run on one before-and-after scene. Equation discovery with PySINDy or PySR is the first thing to cut.

### (e) Detective and skeptic agents: tools compute, the model narrates

The Gemini API's function calling lets the model call the team's own Python tools, and its documentation says Gemini 3 and 4 series models can combine built-in tools with custom functions in one turn, in preview ([Gemini API tools docs](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/tools)). There is no first-party Earth Engine tool, and Google's own geospatial agent is gated, so the team builds its own; the Earth AI post is a design reference ([Putting Earth AI to work](https://research.google/blog/putting-earth-ai-to-work-geospatial-agents-for-our-planets-biggest-challenges/)). Academic precedents exist: Earth-Agent (ICLR 2026) uses 104 tools and reports that general agents often failed completely on some task groups ([Earth-Agent project page](https://opendatalab.github.io/Earth-Agent)).

The design rule comes from what goes wrong. Data-analysis agent benchmarks, seen only as search snippets, group failures into wrong plans, wrong data selection, wrong implementation and cascading state errors, with the best model on one benchmark at 59.43 pass@1 **[snippet]** ([AgenticDataBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.01647); [arXiv 2603.15636](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.15636v1)). No source was found that quantifies fabricated statistics specifically. The mitigation is structural: a small fixed set of typed tools (load region, compute trend, compute false-discovery map, compute resilience indicator, run PCMCI), every number in the narrative pulled from a logged tool result and never typed by the model, and a skeptic whose checklist is the trap table below, re-running each finding with a different test, period and dataset. A provenance log the judges can open is the feature that distinguishes Canary from a chatbot. Gemini free-tier limits and current model IDs were not confirmed.

## Conclusion

The survey changes where a team should expect to spend its 48 hours. Data access, usually the first day's sink, collapses to minutes when the design starts from POWER, Open-Meteo, PSL files and plain-text indices and treats Earthdata and Earth Engine as pre-loaded supplements. Compute collapses too: a compiled kernel turns global significance maps into a coffee-break job. What does not collapse is the distance between a pretty map and a defensible one, and that is exactly where this challenge is judged. The null tests give Canary its sharpest asset: a team that can show the 15% and 80% false-alarm rates of the naive methods, then show its own corrected maps, demonstrates the "detective" skill the challenge title asks for more convincingly than any stretch layer.

The stretch layers work best as one argument, not five features. Two independent reanalyses agreeing on a trend, a single-sensor resilience indicator with surrogate significance, a basin-scale water balance with honest error bars, a small causal graph that names ENSO during what the index files show is a very strong El Niño, a forecast that reports whether it beat seasonal-naive, and a skeptic agent that re-runs every claim: each is a way of saying "we tried to break this result and here is what survived". The open question the team should resolve first is the one this research could not: what NASA itself lists on the challenge's Resources tab, since a stack that ignores a recommended dataset invites the first question from the judges.
